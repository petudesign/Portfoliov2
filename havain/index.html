Havain

Havain turns grocery shelves into structured data. An employee sweeps a shelf with a phone, computer vision builds a model of it from the shelf-edge labels and what sits above them, and GS1 2D barcodes add batches and expiry dates.

2D codes tell what and when. Vision tells where. Scans make it true. The first use case is expiry and waste in fresh departments.

My role
Product design, research and development
Timeline
Nov 2026 – in progress
Status
Simulation, phone prototype and maintenance centre demo on a simulated store; computer vision in progress

What Havain is meant to become

The goal

A system that keeps every shelf in a grocery store mapped and current: what is on it, where, and until when. Employees capture shelves with a phone during their normal rounds, and department managers and retailers see in one place what needs attention: near-expiry batches, gaps, wrong products and shelves that need facing.

Where it starts

Expiry and waste in fresh departments, in Finnish grocery retail. It’s built on global standards, GS1 2D barcodes and GTINs, so it isn’t tied to one chain or one country.

Early days. Havain is at the start of development. Everything on this page, the screenshots included, will change as it’s built and tested.

Start from S-hävikki’s gap

Problem

My 2025 concept, S-hävikki, had one big gap: an EAN barcode doesn’t carry the expiry date, so checking dates stays manual.

What changed

GS1 2D barcodes can carry the batch and expiry date, and retail is moving to read them at checkout. Sunrise 2027 is an industry goal, not a law, and Finland is in the pilot stage.

Test the idea before building it

What I did

A day-by-day simulation of one dairy shelf, comparing four ways of finding near-expiry products: today’s walk-through routine, a manually updated shelf map (the 2025 concept), a shelf map with 2D data, and the same plus sales speed.

Assumptions

Most parameters are assumptions, and they’re listed on the simulation page. The results below are model results, not field measurements.

My shop-floor experience corrected the model

What the model said

The first version said labelling every near-expiry product would lower the margin.

What I knew

After four years in a hypermarket dairy department, I knew fast sellers almost never get close to their date. The model had an unrealistic shelf: fast sellers reached the label window on 15–19% of days. Once fixed, about 2%, and the result disappeared.

Principle

A model is only as good as the shelf it imagines. Domain knowledge decides which results to trust.

What held up

Missed products

In the simulation, today’s routine missed 5–14 near-expiry units per shelf per week across a range of new-employee skill and speed. The data map missed none.

Why it matters

With the data map, the result no longer depends on who is working. Waste per shelf fell by about a third, in the model.

Model results under stated assumptions, not field measurements.

Design for the newest person on the shift

Why

Retail has high staff turnover, so someone on every shift is still learning the store, and people read and work in different ways. The tool has to work for all of them, starting with someone in their first week.

Rules

Tasks one at a time, plain language, never colour alone, undo everywhere, “I can’t find these” as a normal answer, and tasks scoped by role.

Phone prototype

One scan, two jobs: receive a new batch, or confirm the right product for a task. GS1 Digital Link QR codes open it straight from the iPhone camera. Demo codes use the GS1 example prefix 952, so no real product codes.

Tested so far

In a desktop browser at phone width, in Finnish and English. It hasn’t been tested on a real iPhone in a store yet.

From expiry tool to platform

The assumption

The simulation assumed a shelf map exists and stays correct. In a real store someone has to build it and keep it up to date as the assortment changes, and that is the job for computer vision.

Key idea

Identity comes from the shelf-edge label; vision only checks what is above it. Identifying any of thousands of products becomes checking whether this is the product the label says.

Two products, one data

A maintenance centre on the desktop, with the store map, shelf front views and a review queue for what vision is unsure about, and a field app on the phone for scans, tasks and guided capture. The maintenance centre runs as a demo on a simulated store; it isn’t on real store data yet.

What’s next, and what isn’t proven

Next

Capture real shelves with the store’s permission, measure what vision recognises, and publish each validation step’s result.

Not proven yet

No real shelf has been captured and no recognition accuracy has been measured. No retailer has been approached, on purpose: the proof comes first.
