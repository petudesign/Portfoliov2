Helsinki Sports Map 2026

Find a place to play in Helsinki. See how to get there.

A mobile-focused sports map covering all of Helsinki, bringing venue discovery and route planning into one place for residents and visitors.

Product design, development & data integration

Problem

Venue discovery is fragmented.

Finding a sport can mean several searches and a visit to each venue’s website for practical details.

My contribution

Connect discovery, routes and source data.

I designed and built the discovery flow and integrations, changed the map implementation when optimisation consumed too much time, and created a queue for manually checking venue data.

Find a place, then get there

Bring the venue and the journey together

The idea came from the effort of finding somewhere to exercise: scattered search results, incomplete information and more searching on venue websites. I wanted residents and visitors to find an activity and work out how to get there in one place.

I prioritised mobile because I expect people to use the map while choosing a destination or moving around Helsinki. Sport filters, address search, optional current location and venue details form the discovery flow. A linked text-based list can be used by keyboard and screen reader, so venues remain browsable without selecting map markers.

Replace the custom map to focus on the product

I first built a custom renderer for a distinctive visual experience. As the dataset grew, responsive zooming and rendering became a substantial development task. Spatial chunks, background rendering in a worker and reused frames helped, but optimisation continued to consume time.

I switched to MapLibre GL JS with OpenFreeMap’s Positron vector basemap, using its touch gestures, clustering and 2D or tilted views.

My priority: put development time into helping people find a suitable place and get there. Maintaining the custom visual engine was taking too much time from that goal.

Use the right source for each part of the journey

Walking, cycling and driving routes sit alongside public transport through HSL’s Digitransit routing API. Helsinki’s Paikkatietohaku provides address lookup through separately requested API access; the city’s WFS street index supports address suggestions.

Venue data comes from LIPAS, enriched with Helsinki Service Map information. These sources provide citywide coverage, but completeness varies by field. Free use can be confirmed from available data; paid access and exact prices need further checking.

Review discrepancies before treating them as facts

I wanted people to make a practical decision from the information shown. A missing price could not be treated as free access, so I built a manual review queue. It separates incoming venue data from the information I am prepared to approve.

  1. Compare: check extracted information against the official venue source.
  2. Review: accept, correct or reject a candidate when the information does not agree.
  3. Export: save review decisions locally and export approved records as JSON. Approval does not automatically publish a record to the map.

Price conditions matter too: an adult ticket, a concession and an equipment charge represent different costs. Current work focuses on making those details and venue accessibility information available, so people can judge suitability before travelling.

Make Helsinki the first city, with room for others

City-specific configuration and data providers are separated from the interface and renderer. That makes expansion a matter of adapting sources and configuration rather than rebuilding the application.

A Chinese-speaking friend has helped with translations toward Chinese-language support for visitors. Longer term, I want a trending layer based on recent interest. That needs enough real activity and a clear measurement window; the current preview is illustrative.
