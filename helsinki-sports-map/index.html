Helsinki Sports Map

Finding somewhere to play a sport in Helsinki meant several searches, a visit to each venue’s website and then another app for the route. I built one map for finding a venue and getting there.

I designed the discovery flow, built it and its data integrations with coding agents, and review venue data myself before it’s shown as fact.

Bring the venue and the journey together

Problem

Finding a sport meant scattered search results, incomplete information and more searching on venue websites, then planning the trip somewhere else.

What I did

One panel holds sport filters, Near me, local search and venue details that lead straight into walking, cycling, driving and HSL routes. Search runs locally on every keystroke and parses combined sport and district queries, Finnish inflections and local slang like Sörkka, Laru or Itis. A linked text list stays usable with a keyboard and screen reader.

Mobile first

I expect people to use the map while choosing a destination or already moving around Helsinki, so the phone came first. Saved favourites stay in the browser with no account, and the Saved filter chip only appears once you star your first place.

Desktop

The list first opened as a drawer over the map on every screen. On desktop it became a fixed column beside the map, so browsing and seeing where venues are happen at once; phones keep the drawer.

Meet halfway directly in search

Design decision

When meeting a friend across Helsinki, people want a court or hall that is roughly equal distance for both. I first tried a separate Meet halfway panel and filter chip, which added clutter and sat hidden past the sport filters. Removing the extra controls and building the feature into the main search bar kept the interface clean.

How it works

Type two Helsinki districts into search, including local nicknames like “Kallio and Lauttasaari” or “koris Kallio ja Laru”. The map marks both starting areas (A and B) with a dashed line between them, and the list ranks venues fairest first by minimizing the longer trip and penalizing lopsided splits, with colour-coded distances for both friends.

Cut mobile chrome after testing on a phone

Problem

Testing on an iPhone 11 showed the HSL zone card, route inputs and map legend taking up more than 60% of the map. Opening the bottom sheet also let it slide underneath the floating route card at the top.

What I did

Once a route resolves, the planner collapses into a single 3-row card: an editable start-to-destination bar matching the map markers, a travel-mode switch with a sliding thumb, and a compact AB zone badge with distance. Step-by-step HSL directions, the 100 € ticket reminder and the map legend moved into the bottom sheet, following the map pattern people already know.

Sheet bounds and timeline

A live ResizeObserver tracks the bottom edge of the top card so the expanded sheet always stops 8 px below it, keeping the drag handle and header visible. Inside the sheet, a vertical timeline mirrors the map’s line styling (green dashed for walking, solid blue for transit), with duplicate leg pills and nested card borders removed.

Small mobile fixes

The language switch used to sit in the list footer behind 60 venues; moving it into the Filters menu put it one tap away without squeezing the search bar. Bottom-sheet drag math was also adjusted to respect iOS safe-area insets.

Replace the custom map to focus on the product

Problem

I first built a custom renderer for a distinctive look. As the dataset grew, smooth zooming and rendering became a project of its own.

What helped, for a while

Spatial chunks, rendering in a background worker and reused frames helped, but optimisation kept taking time away from the product.

Decision

I switched to MapLibre GL JS with OpenFreeMap’s Positron basemap, with its touch gestures, clustering and 2D or tilted views.

Trade-off

The switch gave up some character in exchange for the whole city, smooth zooming and time for helping people find a place and get there.

Use the right source for each part of the journey

Routes

Walking, cycling and driving routes sit alongside public transport from HSL’s Digitransit routing API, with fare zones and ticket reminders.

Venues

LIPAS provides the citywide sports registry. I classified 394 generic ball-field records from their descriptions (bringing searchable football venues to 285), widened sports-park clustering from 80 m to 450 m to remove duplicate parks, and expanded the Helsinki Service Map import from 256 to 1,065 units for photos, opening hours and accessibility.

Addresses

Helsinki’s Paikkatietohaku provides address lookup through separately requested API access, and the city’s street index supports suggestions.

Prices

Free use can be confirmed from the data. Paid access and exact prices need checking, which is what the review queue is for.

Review discrepancies before treating them as facts

Problem

People should be able to make a practical decision from what the map shows. A missing price can’t be shown as free access.

What I did

A manual review queue keeps incoming venue data apart from what I’m prepared to approve. I compare it with the official source, accept, correct or reject it, and export approved records. Approval never publishes anything by itself.

Evidence, not probability

Each candidate gets a rule-based evidence score: a recognised price format, a match to the LIPAS venue and a fresh source add points, while an unfinished cross-check holds points back.

Next

Accessibility details are now imported for 192 units from the Service Map, and price conditions remain the main gap: an adult ticket, a concession and an equipment charge are different costs.

How I worked with agents

Split

Agents mapped the open data sources and their terms, LIPAS, Helsinki Service Map and Digitransit, and built the integrations. I designed the flow for someone navigating Helsinki, decided when to drop the custom map and review venue data myself.

Simplifying on device

Building fast with agents makes it easy to pile controls onto the screen. Testing on my own phone caught what to cut: moving Meet halfway out of its own panel into search, collapsing four floating route bars into one card and keeping saved places hidden until you star one.

Make Helsinki the first city, with room for others

Built to expand

City-specific configuration and data providers are separate from the interface, so another city means adapting sources rather than rebuilding the app.

Visitors

A Chinese-speaking friend has helped with translations towards Chinese support for visitors. A trending layer would need enough real activity first; the current preview is illustrative.
