One place to find a sport

The idea came from a simple frustration: finding somewhere to exercise could mean several searches, an incomplete result and another visit to a venue’s own website for the details. I wanted to bring discovery and practical information into one place.

Helsinki Sports Map now covers the whole city. The aim is to help residents and visitors find a suitable activity without already knowing the local venues or where their information is published.

Mobile discovery, followed by a real route

I have prioritised mobile usability because I expect people to use the map while deciding where to go or already moving around the city. Sport filters, address search, optional current location and venue details form the core discovery flow. The map is paired with a text-based venue list that can be navigated by keyboard and read by screen readers, so finding a place does not depend on seeing or selecting map markers.

Routing connects the result to the journey: walking, cycling and driving routes sit alongside public transport through HSL’s Digitransit routing API. Helsinki’s Paikkatietohaku handles address lookup using separately requested API access, supported by the city’s WFS street index for address suggestions.

Giving up the custom renderer

I first built a custom map renderer because I wanted a distinctive visual experience. As the geographic dataset grew, keeping zooming and rendering responsive became a major task. I tried spatial chunks, background rendering in a worker and reusing rendered frames, but optimisation kept taking time away from the product itself.

I replaced that renderer with MapLibre GL JS and OpenFreeMap’s Positron vector basemap. The current map supports 2D and tilted views, native touch gestures and clustered venue markers. The decision let me put more development effort into finding places, understanding their details and making the mobile experience useful.

Public data needs human judgement

Venue data comes from LIPAS, with Helsinki Service Map information used for enrichment. Citywide coverage does not mean every detail is complete: the available data can directly confirm free opportunities, while paid access and exact price lists still need more checking. A missing price is not evidence that a venue is free.

I built a review queue to check extracted information against official venue sources. It gives me a place to accept, correct or reject candidates when the data and the source do not agree. The current workflow keeps review decisions locally and exports approved records as JSON; it does not automatically publish them to the map.

Price lists also need their conditions: an adult ticket, a concession rate and an equipment charge cannot all be reduced to the same entry price. This review work is part of making practical venue information trustworthy.

Useful beyond the local audience

I want the map to be a convenient starting point for tourists as well as Helsinki residents. A Chinese-speaking friend has helped with translations toward Chinese-language support, making it easier to serve visitors who would otherwise need to navigate Finnish or English sources.

The architecture separates city-specific configuration and data providers from the interface and map renderer. Helsinki is the first implementation, but adding another city should require adapting its data and configuration rather than rebuilding the whole application.

Next: accessibility, prices and meaningful trends

Current work focuses on showing reliable venue accessibility information and price lists. These details help people judge whether a place actually suits their needs before travelling there.

Longer term, I want a trending map layer to highlight venues receiving recent interest. It would need enough visitors and a clear measurement window to produce a useful signal. The existing preview is illustrative; it is not evidence of real popularity.
