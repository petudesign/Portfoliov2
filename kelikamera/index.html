Kelikamera AI

Six in ten of Fintraffic’s 800 road weather cameras have no surface sensor nearby, so nobody can say whether their stretch of road is slippery. I’m collecting winter data to train a local model to estimate the road surface and its friction straight from the camera image, for the cameras no sensor covers.

It’s in progress. Images and sensor readings for winter 2026–27 are collected about every three hours, and the live map fetches the current sensor readings from Digitraffic every five minutes.

Baseline on 11 November 2026: 475 cameras had no optical road sensor, though 174 of them still sit within 5 km of a weather station measuring road and air temperatures. The aim is to turn the remaining grey points into surface estimates.

Let the weather stations label the images

Problem

Labelling thousands of camera images by hand doesn’t scale, and it would be my guess rather than a measurement.

What I did

Each image is paired with the nearest road weather station’s optical surface sensor: road condition, friction, and water, snow and ice on the surface. Fintraffic doesn’t publish what the sensor codes mean, so I checked them against the amounts measured at the same station. Code 7 came with 1.8 mm of ice and friction 0.11, so it means ice.

What I left out

A second surface sensor would have doubled the coverage, 419 stations instead of 252. Its codes didn’t match any known scheme, and guessing them would have meant training on wrong labels.

Where the labels can be wrong

The nearest station isn’t always near. The median distance is 3.5 km, but one in ten is over 21 km away and the farthest 98 km. The map only shows a station’s reading for a camera within 5 km.

Many cameras also take a picture only once an hour, so a rule of ‘reading within 30 minutes of the image’ threw away 449 of 815 images in one morning run. The collector now matches each image with the reading closest to the moment it was taken, from the station’s 5-minute history.

Temperatures without an optical sensor

174 of the cameras marked “no sensor data” still sit within 5 km of a weather station that reports road and air temperatures. Those readings were hidden before. They now show up in the same unified reading card alongside the road temperature rate of change (°C/h), which signals rapid cooling near freezing and black-ice risk.

A map that is useful before the model exists

Idea

The model needs a winter of data first, so the map had to be worth opening on its own: measured conditions, live traffic and maintenance activity, honestly marked where data is missing.

Live traffic with a strict 5 km limit

Opening a camera fetches live flow, average speed and hourly volume (such as Smooth · 96 km/h · 1,318 veh/h) from Fintraffic’s LAM/TMS stations. Only actively gathering stations within 5 km qualify, so a quiet side road never inherits motorway traffic from a parallel highway. The request (~7 kB) runs only when a camera opens, keeping initial load light.

Lazy map layers and zoom filtering

Two optional layers answer “has this road been salted or ploughed?” and “are there disruptions ahead?”: Salting & ploughing (3 h) and Roadworks & notices. Both stay unloaded until toggled on. Maintenance tracks use magenta (salting), cyan (ploughing) and yellow (gritting) so they stay legible on the dark map. Acute disruptions show across all of Finland, while 400+ long-running roadworks appear only from zoom 8 inward so the national view stays readable. Each notice card lists the nearest cameras within 15 km as clickable links.

Panel cleanup and route passing times

Stacked info boxes were merged into one reading card, camera angles moved to a single draggable row with edge fade (dropping repeated road numbers), and same-road prev/next stepping sits under the title with arrow-key support (← / →). Every camera has a direct ?cam= share link with a copy button. Route search adds four one-click example routes and estimates passing time (~HH:MM) for each camera from OSRM leg times, setting up a future forecast view.

Mobile polish

Real phone use exposed cramped scrolling and an unclear fold chevron. The bottom sheet height dropped from 72 dvh to 62 dvh with proper flex scrolling (min-height: 0 and safe-area padding) so the map stays visible above it. The chevron became a quick search button that opens a compact 157 px search bar without covering the map. All inputs are 16 px to prevent iOS auto-zoom, and the data credits sit inside a collapsible “Data sources · Fintraffic, FMI, OSM” row.

Rules for the interface

My own estimates never look like official warnings: the Finnish Meteorological Institute’s warnings are shown separately, with the source named. Measured and estimated conditions will always look different: a filled point for a measurement, a ring and a confidence for an estimate.

Native macOS desktop widget

Why native WidgetKit

Checking a commute camera before leaving shouldn’t mean opening a browser tab and zooming into a map. A native SwiftUI and WidgetKit app puts a favourite camera directly on the Mac desktop (and shares the same widget target for iOS).

Same visual language

The widgets mirror the web app: top condition glow, fresh camera image with capture time, friction gauge, 24-hour condition strip, road and air temperatures, and live traffic across Small (2×2), Medium (4×2) and Large (4×4) sizes.

Configuration and deep links

AppIntents lets you pick or switch the camera directly from widget settings without opening the host app. Tapping any widget opens that exact camera on the live web map via its ?cam= link.

Making the interface hold up

I ran three rounds on the interface, each with a measurable end.

Break it

I rebuilt the page with worst-case data: overlong place names, 30 warnings, 169 cameras turning icy at once, no measurements at all, and broken images. I tested it at 320 px, the narrowest phone width. Six things broke. The update time wrapped onto three lines on an ordinary phone, and one long name filled the whole list.

Test with simulated users

Five agents each used the map as one person, with a device, a task and a time limit, and reported where they got stuck. They aren’t real users, so this is early feedback, not user testing. One finding from each:

  1. Road maintenance duty officerDesktop · Vaasa–Seinäjoki at 3 am

    A camera marked icy, with good friction. The page was right: the station’s optical sensor reported ice, but only 0.02 mm of it.

    What changedThe panel now says the sensor sees a thin layer and friction is still good. It turned out to be common: that morning 7 of 8 snow cameras still had good friction. Thin layers are now flagged and left out of the first model’s classes, and the model also learns friction, so “slippery” comes from friction, not from the class name. On the map, a thin layer is now its own class and doesn’t count as slippery.

  2. Low-vision driver200 % zoom · VoiceOver · red-green colour blind

    With deuteranopia, ice and dry came out almost the same colour. Map points couldn’t be reached by keyboard at all, and opening a camera didn’t move focus.

    What changedIce points have a white ring, so ice no longer depends on colour alone. A camera search reaches any camera by keyboard. Opening a panel moves focus to its heading, and closing it returns focus to where it was.

  3. Hiring design leadDesktop · 2 minutes, in English

    Couldn’t tell what the project was for. A grey camera’s “AI estimate coming” read as unfinished.

    What changedThe map panel opens with why six in ten cameras are grey: “I’m training an AI to estimate their conditions from the image.” A grey camera now says “No sensor nearby · see the camera image”.

  4. DispatcherDesktop · planning night routes

    The route summary counted warnings starting on Saturday as being on the route today.

    What changedWarnings in force now or within 12 hours are counted apart from ones that start later.

  5. CommuterPhone · 60 seconds before leaving

    Opening a camera didn’t move the map to it. Two other personas ran into the same thing.

    What changedThe map glides to the camera, and on a phone it stops above the panel.

Measure it

I ran Lighthouse, fixed what it found, and measured again. Moving the map library out of the first paint brought mobile performance from 82 to 100, with the first paint going from 3.6 to 0.8 s. Accessibility went from 90 to 100.

Check the labels before trusting them

Blind check

Before training, I labelled 300 images blind, without seeing the sensor’s answer. Image and sensor agreed on winter or not 79% of the time, but on the exact class only 41%: ice almost always looked like snow, and dry, damp and wet blurred together.

What it changed

The model’s main target became winter yes/no plus friction, with the five classes kept as an experiment.

Rules and limits

The first round also showed the review needed a rule: the sensor measures the wheel track, so slush between lanes doesn’t count. I’m red-green colour blind, so I noted that too: this isn’t a neutral human ceiling.

Measure the claim, not just the coverage

Rule

Coverage alone proves nothing: a model that guesses “dry” everywhere would cover every camera. Coverage is always reported together with accuracy.

How it will be measured

On cameras the model has never seen: how many slippery roads it catches, and how often a “slippery” warning is right.

The headline I’m aiming for

“Road conditions for 800 cameras instead of 325, with slippery roads recognised X% of the time on cameras the model has never seen.” X stays empty until it’s measured.

Collect the winter first

Where it stands

In early November the winter classes were nearly empty: ice or frost on 2 cameras and snow on 3. By 13 November the first snowfall had pushed snow past 30 cameras, and ice was at about 18. Every class passed 30 cameras on 17 November.

Free to run

Collection runs on GitHub Actions and the data goes to a Hugging Face dataset, so it costs nothing. Weather forecasts are archived alongside.

The image archive stays private: the pictures show vehicles and sometimes people, and a public archive would keep them forever. The map itself only shows Fintraffic’s own last 24 hours.

Collect by the clock, not by the run

Problem

GitHub started the hourly schedule only 3 times in 21 hours. Many cameras also take a picture only once an hour, so the rule “sensor reading within 30 minutes of the image” threw away about half of the images.

What I did

Digitraffic keeps about 23 hours of history for both camera images and sensor readings. The collector now fills in fixed moments every three hours afterwards. For each moment it takes the image closest to that moment, and the sensor reading closest to when that image was taken. A dropped run no longer loses data.

If no run gets through within that window, a check flags the lost moment and GitHub sends an alert. A heartbeat keeps GitHub from switching the schedule off after 60 quiet days, which the first version would never have done.

Between those moments it adds hourly images, but only from cameras where the station measures snow, ice or a road surface near freezing. Those are the classes the model is still missing.

Result

The image is within 5 minutes of the moment, and the sensor reading within 2.5 minutes of the image. Before, the gap could be up to 30 minutes. The winter’s data comes to about 60 GB, under the 100 GB limit. Four days in: 1.7 GB.

On the map

The timeline now shows the camera image from the chosen hour as well. A red point at 3 am comes with the 3 am picture.

GitHub ran the hourly job only every 4–10 hours, so readings were hours old. Now the browser fetches the current readings itself; the collection stays on the schedule.

Why a local model

Why not an AI API

A vision API would cost money for every one of about 19,000 images a day, give different answers whenever the provider updates its model, and hide what the model looked at. A small local model runs free on GitHub Actions, gives the same answer for the same image, and can be measured on cameras it has never seen.

Which model

A frozen DINOv2 (small) as the feature extractor, with a small trained head that outputs both the road condition and its friction. It copes with little winter data and runs on a CPU. A fully fine-tuned EfficientNet-B0 is the baseline it has to beat.

What comes next

Next

With every class past 30 cameras, next come the model’s estimate for the grey cameras, a heatmap of what the model looked at in the image, and a three-day slippery-road risk per road section. For that, every collection already archives the weather forecasts, so a model can later learn how forecasts turn into actual road conditions.

Accidents

I’m also archiving Fintraffic’s accident reports (about 4,200 in the past year) to ask whether accidents are more common on slippery roads. A raw count says nothing, so the comparison will be per hour of driving, at the same places and times of day, slippery hours against grippy ones, scaled by traffic volume.

How I corrected the agent: what the page tells third parties

What happened

The agent loaded the font from Google Fonts and the map library from a public CDN. That is the fastest way to add them, and nobody had asked who receives the visitor’s data. I noticed it and asked whether the live map was leaking anything.

The audit

Every request sends at least the visitor’s IP address, browser and the page URL. Google and the CDN got all three on every page load, and the page needed neither. A German court ruled in 2022 that loading Google Fonts from Google without consent breaks GDPR.

The remaining services are needed for the map to work: map tiles, Fintraffic’s camera images, place search and routing. They now stay, and the page says what each of them sees.

The fix

The font and map library are now served from the site itself. A short privacy note sits at the bottom of the page: no cookies, no analytics.

Making it stick

A guard script opens the page, opens a camera and lists every external server the browser contacts. Any server not on the allowed list fails the check. Run against the old version, it flags Google Fonts and the CDN, so the same slip can’t come back unnoticed.

As a side effect, mobile load got faster: the largest element now shows up in 1.1 s instead of 1.5 s.

What I noticed that the tests didn’t

Two cameras were both marked “snow / slush”. In Ilmajoki the road looked almost bare: friction 0.69, a layer of 0.06 mm. In Veteli it was clearly snowy: friction 0.34, 0.15 mm. The training data already set thin layers apart, but the map didn’t, so it showed something different from what the model would learn. Now the map uses the same rule as the training data: a thin layer with good grip is its own class, and it doesn’t count as slippery on the timeline, routes or the slippery-roads list.

How I worked with agents

Decision log

I make the calls and the agents carry them out. Each decision goes into a log with the reason and the option it ruled out, such as leaving out the second sensor whose codes couldn’t be verified. Agents read the log before they start, so a rejected idea doesn’t return in a later session.

When an agent chose convenience over privacy by loading the font from Google, the fix became a rule: any new external server has to be justified and added to an allowed list.

Split

Agents built the collector, the labels and the map. I’m responsible for how it looks and how easy it is to use, and I set the rules for what the project may claim, checking every change on desktop and phone.

Agents as testers

Agents also tested the map, each as one person in a set role. What they found is under Making the interface hold up.
