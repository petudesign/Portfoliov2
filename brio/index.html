Brio 2026

Set a scrolling limit. Complete your chosen activity to continue.

An iOS app concept designed to track only the apps a user chooses. At the planned 20-minute limit, those apps are blocked until the user completes their chosen activity. App blocking is still in development.

My role
Product strategy, research, design & development
Problem

Scrolling continues without a decision.

People who want help limiting automatic scrolling need a boundary they can choose, while still being able to return to the apps they value.

My contribution

Building the product and the business.

I have done most of the research, design and implementation, using builder, product-management and research agents to support the work.

Current state

Core screens built; app blocking in progress.

Onboarding is nearly finished, the main-screen UI is largely complete and MediaPipe push-up tracking is implemented. Other movements and app blocking are still unfinished.

How Brio is designed to work

When enabled by the user, Brio runs in the background and tracks usage of selected apps only. After a planned 20 minutes of use, it blocks those apps until the user completes an activity they have chosen. Completing it unlocks another period of use; reaching the limit again leads to a longer or more demanding activity.

The progression is bounded: it resets within 24 hours and eases after longer breaks during the day. Brio offers guidance to people who opt in, without prompting them to return.

My first App Store product

Brio is my most demanding commercial project so far. I am working on the business as well as the app, with particular attention to competitor research and onboarding psychology. I want to grow recurring revenue through an ethical experience that respects why people use the product. I have also planned the analytics layer and refined the marketing plan. After the App Store release, I intend to A/B test screens and features to find which combinations work best.

Beyond push-ups and squats

The first idea used push-ups or squats as the break. I quickly realised that this would exclude people with limited mobility who also struggle with doomscrolling. The direction now includes a short, Guitar Hero-inspired activity with timed movement prompts, with movement options still to be developed.

A break that does not become another habit

The activity should create a pause, not a reason to keep playing. I am considering even the edge case of someone leaving social media open or scrolling more just to trigger the game. How to make the break engaging without encouraging repeat play is still an open design question.

A boundary the user chooses

Brio is designed to run in the background when the user enables it, tracking only the apps they select and their usage. The planned starting limit is 20 minutes of use. When that limit is reached, the selected apps are blocked until the user completes their preferred activity.

Completing the activity unlocks another period of use. If the user reaches the next 20-minute limit, the required activity becomes longer or more demanding. This gives repeated scrolling a gradually increasing pause while keeping a route back to the apps.

The progression does not grow indefinitely. It resets within 24 hours and also steps down after longer breaks during the day. For example, after eight hours away from the selected apps, the next requirement should be easier than the one encountered before the break. The precise progression and recovery rules are still being developed.

Brio is not a coach directing the user's day. It offers a boundary to people who choose that guidance, and does not ask them to come back. Choosing to stop using the selected apps should never create a reason to return.

My first product intended for the App Store

Brio is the first product I intend to release on the App Store, making it my most demanding project in building a real business so far. The work includes the product experience, implementation, positioning and a path to recurring revenue.

I want the business to grow in a way that respects the user's goal of spending less time scrolling. That also constrains monetisation: I do not want to use revenue tactics that undermine that goal simply to increase monthly recurring revenue.

Researching the product and its first impression

I have put particular effort into research, competitor comparisons and onboarding psychology because Brio needs to become a viable commercial product. Exploring onboarding made me appreciate how much the first experience can influence a person's decision to continue or pay. I have not yet measured Brio's own conversion.

I have also planned the analytics layer and refined the marketing plan. Once Brio is on the App Store, I intend to A/B test screens and features, comparing how different combinations support onboarding, conversion and the goal of more intentional scrolling. These experiments are planned; they have not run yet.

There are competing products, but in my research I did not find an exact match for the approach I wanted: helping people set limits while recognising that social media can also be useful and enjoyable. That perspective guides the language and the kind of interruption I am designing.

Push-ups and squats were too narrow

My initial idea was straightforward: interrupt scrolling with push-ups or squats. I quickly realised that those requirements would exclude people who cannot comfortably perform them. Someone whose legs do not move as expected can still struggle with doomscrolling.

This changed the direction from a fixed exercise requirement toward a broader set of movement options. Accessibility here affects the core activity, not just the interface. Those alternatives are still being developed; the existing push-up tracker does not yet make the experience suitable for everyone.

Designing a short activity without encouraging more play

The current direction is a small Guitar Hero-inspired activity: movement prompts arrive on screen and the user performs them at the right moment. The intention is to make a brief physical pause clear and engaging.

That creates its own tension. If the activity becomes something people want to replay, it could encourage them to leave social media open or scroll more just to trigger another round. Even though this is an edge case, I want to account for it in the design. The activity is a way to unlock the selected apps after a deliberate pause. Encouraging repeat play would work against that purpose.

This remains a design direction, not a finished or validated minigame.

Doing most of the work, with agent support

I originally planned to build Brio with a friend. Their work commitments have limited their availability, so I have done most of the work so far.

I have used agents in builder, product-management and research roles, giving me support across implementation, planning and investigation. I direct that work and make the product decisions, bringing the research, design and development together.

What is implemented today

  • Onboarding: nearly complete, with final refinements remaining.
  • Main screen: largely complete at the UI level.
  • Movement tracking: push-up support implemented with Google's MediaPipe; other movements remain to be added.
  • App blocking: still in progress and the largest unfinished implementation task.

The App Store release is a goal, not the current state. The app-blocking flow, activity progression and recovery rules still need implementation and evaluation, alongside the broader movement options.
