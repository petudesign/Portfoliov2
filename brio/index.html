Brio 2026

Set a scrolling limit. Complete your chosen activity to continue.

An iOS app concept designed to track only the apps a user chooses. At the planned 20-minute limit, those apps are blocked until the user completes their chosen activity. App blocking is still in development.

My role
Product strategy, research, design & development
Problem

Scrolling continues on autopilot.

People who want limits need a boundary they choose, with a clear way to return to the apps they value.

My contribution

Product strategy through implementation.

I lead the product direction and have done most of the research, design and development. Builder, PM and research agents support the work; product decisions remain with me.

Current state

Core UI built; blocking unfinished.

Onboarding is nearly finished, the main-screen UI is largely complete and MediaPipe push-up tracking works. App blocking and other activities are still in development.

The planned loop

Selected apps reach a planned 20-minute limit, then remain blocked until the user completes a chosen activity. Repeated limits require more effort, with a bounded progression that resets within 24 hours and eases after longer breaks.

Why the activity changed

Push-ups and squats would exclude people with limited mobility. I moved toward more movement options and a short Guitar Hero-inspired activity. It must create a pause without becoming a reason to scroll more or seek another round.

Building a business around the same goal

Brio is my first product intended for the App Store. Competitor and onboarding research inform the experience and commercial plan. Analytics and post-release A/B tests are planned; growth needs to respect the user’s intention to spend less time scrolling.

Make the boundary voluntary and predictable

Brio is designed to run in the background when enabled, tracking only apps the user selects. The starting limit is planned at 20 minutes. At that point, completing a preferred activity would unlock another period of use.

Reaching the limit again would require a longer or more demanding activity. The progression is bounded: it resets within 24 hours and steps down after longer breaks. After eight hours away, for example, the next requirement should be easier than the previous one. These rules and app blocking are still being developed.

The user chooses whether to accept this guidance. Brio does not prompt them to return; stopping use should support their goal.

Let accessibility change the core activity

The first idea was to ask for push-ups or squats. I soon realised that people who cannot comfortably perform those movements can also struggle with doomscrolling. A fixed exercise requirement would exclude them from the core experience.

The decision: broaden the activities people can choose. People with limited mobility also experience doomscrolling, so the initial push-up and squat requirement needed to change.

The MediaPipe push-up tracker is implemented; the other activities remain to be developed. The direction has changed, while the broader experience is still unfinished.

Make the pause engaging without rewarding repeat play

The current concept is a short Guitar Hero-inspired activity, with movement prompts performed at the right time. It should make the pause clear and manageable.

A game people want to replay could work against that purpose. I am considering the edge case of someone keeping social media open or scrolling more to trigger another round. That tension guides the activity design; the minigame is not yet finished or validated.

Research the first experience and the business together

Brio is my first product intended for the App Store. I have invested in competitor comparisons, onboarding psychology, positioning and a marketing plan. The research made me pay closer attention to how the first experience can influence continuation and payment; I have not measured Brio’s conversion yet.

I found competing products, but no exact match for the approach I wanted: social media can be useful and enjoyable, while boundaries can make its use more intentional. That informs the language and interruption. It also constrains monetisation: increasing recurring revenue should respect the reason someone uses Brio.

This creates a product and business question: how can Brio earn continued use while supporting the user’s wish to spend less time in the selected apps? The same tension appears in the activity itself, which needs to be engaging enough to complete without becoming another reason to seek screen time.

The analytics layer is planned. After release, I intend to A/B test screens and features against onboarding, conversion and more intentional use. Those experiments have not run yet. The aim is to find a combination that works commercially and remains consistent with the voluntary guidance people signed up for.

Bring research, design and development together

I originally planned to build Brio with a friend. Their work commitments limited their availability, so I have done most of the work. I use builder, product-management and research agents across implementation, planning and investigation. I set the direction and priorities, make the product decisions and bring their work into the same research, design and development process.

  • Onboarding: nearly complete, with final refinements remaining.
  • Main screen: largely complete at the UI level.
  • Movement tracking: MediaPipe push-up support implemented; other movements pending.
  • App blocking: the largest unfinished implementation task.

Release depends on completing and evaluating blocking, activity progression, recovery rules and the broader movement choices. Brio remains an iOS app concept in development.
