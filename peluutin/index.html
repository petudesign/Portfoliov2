From a coach's own problem

My girlfriend and her colleague coach junior football. They needed to stay on top of individual playing time and give parents a clear record. That became Peluutin's first job: track minutes and substitutions during the match, then keep the information for review and Excel export.

Both coaches have tested the product and given feedback throughout development. Her colleague had struggled with another app; trying it myself revealed inconsistent interactions and too many menus. I focused on keeping the main actions together and reducing navigation.

Complete a substitution in one place

The pitch shows formations and player-specific minutes, with the bench below it. Coaches can select several players for a change. At one tester's request, I also added saved lineups so coaches can prepare groups in advance and substitute an entire group together.

On mobile, the full pitch and bench do not fit comfortably together. Selecting a substitute brings the on-field players into a compact grid beside the decision. The coach chooses who comes off without scrolling back to the pitch.

Goals, scorers and match history stay in the same workflow. A statistics page brings together each player's all-time playing time, helping coaches review minutes across matches and explain their distribution to parents.

Decisions shaped by real constraints

  • Keep substitutions together. The mobile pitch and bench could not fit comfortably in one view. The outgoing-player grid keeps the decision beside the bench.
  • Plan on desktop, view on mobile. Detailed exercise editing needs more space. Shared playback works on a phone and opens without team setup.
  • Share without an account. Encoding the exercise in the link makes direct viewing possible today. The cost is long URLs. Team data stays in the browser, with file backups available.

From tracking matches to planning exercises

The coaches then needed a training planner. The software my girlfriend used through the Finnish Football Association would not run on her newer M-series Mac. I tried it on my own computer and wanted to make the experience clearer and more visual.

I built a Three.js exercise planner with 2D and 3D views, an animation timeline and shareable playback. Coaches can add players, training elements, routes and annotations, then use the timeline to set the order of passes and movements.

Create on a larger screen. Watch on a phone.

Coaches can share a PNG or a link to a playable, read-only exercise. Recipients open it directly without creating a team, adding players or entering the editor. Exercise creation uses a larger screen; viewing also works on mobile.

Use feedback and data to improve the flows

I built a PostHog analytics layer to follow visitor numbers, key actions and recurring problems. It helps identify where users struggle and which flows need refinement, alongside feedback from the coaches.

Analytics data is collected only from visitors who opt in through the consent popup.

Working today, still growing

There is no cloud storage yet. Data is kept in the browser, but coaches can also save it as a file on their phone, keep a local backup or transfer it to a computer. Exercises can be saved as files too. A new browser or cleared storage does not automatically recover the data.

Shared links carry the exercise data and bypass setup, at the cost of long URLs. Accounts and PDF export are planned, with shared viewing intended to remain open without signing in.

Football has match management and exercise planning. Futsal currently has its own pitch and match timing, but no exercise planner yet.

The aim is a multi-sport coaching tool, with basketball and floorball planned and ice hockey a possibility. Each sport brings different substitution patterns and timing rules, so expanding means adapting the workflows as well as the pitch. Feedback so far comes from two coaches; broader testing and measured outcomes are still ahead.
