Peluutin 2026

Playing time, substitutions and training plans for junior coaches.

Built with two football coaches. Manage football and futsal matches on mobile, and create shareable 2D/3D football exercises on a larger screen.

Product design & development

Problem

Make playing time explainable.

Two junior coaches needed a record of individual minutes they could review themselves and discuss with parents.

My contribution

Design and development with coaches.

I designed and built the product. Two junior coaches supplied the needs and ongoing feedback that shaped match management and the Three.js exercise planner.

Current state

A working web app.

Football match management and exercise planning are available. Futsal has its own pitch and timing; its exercise planner is still ahead.

A substitution stays in one place

On mobile, choosing a substitute opens a compact grid of on-field players below the bench. A coach can select who comes off without returning to the pitch. Saved lineups, requested by a tester, also let whole groups change together.

Plans people can watch on a phone

Football exercises have a timeline for passes and movement. Coaches create on a larger screen, then share a PNG or playable link that opens on a phone without team setup.

Tablet feedback shaped the editor

One tester’s tablet session led me to keep placement menus open, make elements easier to select and add visible undo and layer controls. I applied the changes to both tablet and desktop. Shared exercises now omit player names and numbers by default, while the original stays unchanged.

Local storage, with file backups

Team data and exercises can be saved to a phone or transferred to a computer. There is no cloud recovery yet. Opt-in PostHog analytics complement coach feedback; expansion into basketball and floorball is planned, with ice hockey a possibility.

Start with the people using it

My girlfriend and her colleague coach junior football. Keeping track of individual minutes was difficult, and they needed to explain playing-time distribution to parents. I made playing-time tracking the first priority, with both coaches testing it throughout development. Their day-to-day needs guided which workflows I built next.

That gave me a direct connection between the product and its intended use. Playing-time records, prepared lineups and exercise planning each came from a coaching task. The relationship let me keep developing with the people who raised the need.

One coach had struggled with a competing app. Trying it myself, I found inconsistent interactions and too many menus. That informed the interface: keep match actions close together and reduce the navigation needed during a game.

Keep the substitution beside the bench

The pitch shows formations and individual minutes, with the bench below. On a phone, both cannot fit comfortably in the same view. I added a compact outgoing-player grid below the bench selection, bringing the players on the pitch to the point where the coach makes the change.

A coach asked to prepare lineups in advance. I turned that request into saved groups that can be substituted together, alongside multiple-player selection on the pitch. This was a concrete change driven by the people using the product.

The statistics page brings together all-time playing minutes. Goals, scorers, match history and Excel export give coaches a record to review after the match.

Expand when a second real need appears

The exercise planner came from a second need raised by the same coaches. The Finnish Football Association software my girlfriend used would not run on her newer M-series Mac. I tried it myself and wanted to make planning clearer and more visual.

I built the football planner with Three.js, 2D/3D views and a timeline that controls the sequence of passes and movement. Players, routes, training elements and annotations form a plan that can be replayed. Detailed editing uses a larger screen; shared playback also works on mobile.

Refine the editor around actual use

All the needs in this development round surfaced while one tester used the exercise planner on a tablet. They also applied to desktop use, so I implemented the changes for both.

  • Keep repeated actions flowing. Coaches can place several cones or other items consecutively, with drawing and placement menus staying open. A grass-only field without goals or pitch markings and an arrow tool give them more ways to describe an exercise.
  • Make selection and recovery easier. I added an Undo button the same size as the other controls and a Layers menu for selecting and deleting elements. Larger hit areas make thin lines easier to select, and area selection now includes drawings. Shapes stay beneath players and equipment, and drawing over them no longer selects them accidentally.
  • Make the sequence match the plan. Passes can target opponents or empty positions. I also corrected action chaining so that an action following a run or dribble starts from the player’s new position.

This feedback turned the next iteration into specific interaction fixes. How well those changes work in use still needs follow-up testing.

Let recipients open the exercise directly

A coach can share a PNG or a read-only playable link. The recipient goes straight to the exercise without creating a team or adding players. The link carries the exercise data, which makes it self-contained but produces long URLs.

Sharing omits player names and numbers by default. This changes only the shared data; the coach’s original exercise stays unchanged.

Team data stays in the browser. File export gives coaches a way to keep backups on their phone or transfer data to a computer; exercises can be saved too. A cleared browser cannot recover that data automatically. Accounts and PDF export are planned, with shared viewing intended to remain open without signing in.

Improve the flows, then adapt to more sports

I added consent-based PostHog analytics to inspect visitor numbers, key actions and recurring problems. Together with coach feedback, it gives me a way to identify flows that need work. Analytics is collected only from visitors who accept the popup.

Football currently includes match management and exercise planning. Futsal has its own pitch and match timing. Basketball and floorball are planned, with ice hockey a possibility.

The next sports will need more than a different pitch. Substitution frequency and timing rules affect how a coach prepares and manages a match, so the football workflow is a starting point that needs adapting for each sport.

Feedback so far comes from two coaches. Their involvement has shaped concrete features, but broader testing is still needed to understand how well the workflows serve other coaching styles. Measured outcomes are still ahead.
