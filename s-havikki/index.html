S-hävikki 2025

Make near-expiry work easier for store employees.

A food waste service concept connecting an employee tool, an in-store point and clearer customer-facing information.

My role
Product, UX & service design
Problem

Expiry checks depend on manual work.

Store employees need to find, handle and hand over near-expiry products during busy daily routines.

My contribution

Connect the employee and customer experience.

I translated more than four years of grocery-retail experience into a shelf-map prototype and a connected service concept, prioritising the employee workflow before deeper app integration.

Current state

A concept with an interactive prototype.

The employee tool illustrates the interaction. Store-system integration, operational feasibility and customer response still need validation.

Map the interface to the physical shelf

Each square represents a shelf section. The proposed status view helps employees see what needs checking and what has been handled, supporting continuity between shifts.

Keep availability promises realistic

Discounted products can sell quickly or already be in a customer’s basket. The proposed app would show broad, time-sensitive category signals; exact future availability and reservations are outside the concept.

Validate the workflow before expanding

The proposed first pilot covers one store and one or two product categories. I would compare shelf-check effort and missed items, then assess the visibility and pickup of a simple branded display. A custom installation and deeper app integration come later if the results justify them.

Start from the work I knew

I worked in grocery retail for more than four years. Expiry checks relied on manual inspection, employee memory, shelf familiarity and local routines. The same shelves could need checking again later, and keeping track of completed work was frustrating when the store was busy.

Discounted products already sell well in many stores. I focused the concept on supporting the work that brings those products to customers: identifying them, handling them consistently and making the remaining selection visible.

Make the shelf the organising structure

I designed the employee tool around a map of the physical shelf. Each square corresponds to one section, with status colours indicating whether it needs attention. A department view supports moving between shelf units. The intended workflow is to check the physical products, decide how to handle them and update the status for the next employee.

I envisage the tool running on handheld scanners or internal tools employees already use. A production version would still need to establish how expiry and product status information reaches it; inventory integration is outside this prototype.

My scope decision: use the first prototype to explore one repeated shelf-checking task and its handover. The operational workflow needs evidence before investment in custom hardware or a deeper app extension.

    Make the in-store point easy to recognise

    The proposed S-hävikki point brings near-expiry products into a consistent cooler, shelf or display near checkout or another high-traffic area. The message is practical: useful food, a clear discount and a changing selection.

    Although I explored a branded installation, the first pilot should use simple signage, consistent labels and an existing product zone. That keeps the setup small enough to find out whether placement and presentation help customers notice and choose the products. S Group is the setting for the concept; the store pilot has not been carried out.

    Match the digital promise to store reality

    The proposed S-kaupat section would show broad, current signals about nearby stores and product categories. Near-expiry stock changes quickly: an item may sell or sit in someone’s basket before availability updates.

    I therefore scoped out reservations and exact future availability. A category signal such as “available today” would need to disappear when the selection is too small or uncertain. The app extension depends on a reliable in-store process and remains secondary to it.

    Define what a useful first pilot would show

    I would begin with one store, one or two product categories and a one-to-two-week pilot. The aim is to assess the employee workflow and customer-facing point before investing in a wider system.

    • Employee workflow: compare ordinary checks with the shelf-map prototype, measuring time, missed items, repeated checks and handover clarity.
    • Customer response: compare the current product location with a branded display, looking at noticeability, pickup and understanding of the offer.
    • Operational fit: track recovered product value, unsold items and whether the process fits normal routines.

    These are planned measures, not results. The central assumption is that the connected workflow and display would improve handling and selection enough to justify the added work. Custom hardware, deeper integrations and personalised alerts would need evidence from that first step.
