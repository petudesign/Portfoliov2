KorisLab

Finnish basketball had no good way to compare teams and players across games. I built one, starting with the women’s Korisliiga to give it more visibility before adding the men’s league.

I designed the product, directed the coding agents that built it and checked the data and models myself.

Let a question lead into the data

Problem

People had to know which view held an answer before they could find it.

What I did

Search finds teams and players, and answers supported season questions with a route into the right profile. Asking who had the most assists in 2025–26 returns player and team rankings, with Jeanae Terry leading on 213, tied to the season and to verified games.

Why it matters

The answer gives people a starting point and a next step into the detail, without first learning how the app is organised.

Next

Widen the range of questions, so an answer can return a figure, open a view or both.

Give every comparison its context

Problem

A comparison without its sample size or units invites the wrong conclusion.

What I did

Matchup Lab compares teams and players across opponents, seasons, recent form and on/off-court periods. A radar chart gives the quick read and a table keeps the original values beside it.

Every comparison type

Team against team, the same team across seasons, the last five games against earlier ones, whole games with a player present or absent, and periods with a player on or off court. For players: player against player, seasons, recent form, and regular season against playoffs.

Principle

Keep the sample, the units and the chart scale visible next to every comparison. The radar’s 0–100 shows league ranks, and its dashed line marks the middle of that scale, not the league average.

What the chart doesn’t say

The profiles describe the available games. A larger radar area doesn’t explain why a team performed differently, and a small sample needs more context before drawing conclusions. Lower defensive ratings and turnover rates are better, so the table keeps the original values and explains their units.

Replay a game, not just its box score

Problem

A box score tells you the final numbers, not how the game turned.

What I did

Game pages replay a game event by event: real shot locations, the running score, player lines and a win probability curve as the timeline, with jumps to the turning points.

Honesty

There is no player tracking, so only shots sit where they were taken. The win probability model was tuned on 2024–25, locked and tested once on 2025–26. It was too confident about home teams in the men’s league, and the page says so below the chart.

Player ratings that show their working

Problem

Per-40 numbers flatter short minutes: 7 points in 5 minutes works out at 56 per 40. My first version also flattened the top, rating players more than 20% apart as 98 and 96.

What I did

Six areas on a 1–99 scale and no overall score, each shown with its real statistic. Short samples are pulled towards a typical regular, and the card shows minutes and a confidence level.

Small samples

The pull grows the shorter a player’s minutes are, up to three times as strong, with 28 minutes a game counting as full. My first version pulled towards the league’s minute-weighted average, which starters dominate, so even a one-minute player rated about 60. It now pulls towards the median regular.

Distance, not rank

Ratings use a fixed scale built from past seasons’ regulars and measure distance from a typical level, with the rank shown separately. 20 rating points equal one standard deviation, so about one regular in 40 reaches 90 in a single area.

Principle

A number is published only with its context, and uncertainty is shown rather than hidden. The box score only records steals and blocks, so that area is called exactly that, not “Defence”.

Sim Studio: play the next game a thousand times

Problem

Statistics pages tell you what happened. I wanted to show how an upcoming game is likely to go, not just who wins.

What I did

Sim Studio plays an upcoming game 1,000 times from official play-by-play, possession by possession: win probability, the spread of the margin, the chance of overtime, each player’s likely points and one simulated game on a 3D court.

How it works

About 600 games over three seasons are rebuilt from play-by-play, and a game is kept only if its points match the official box score. Each game is split into about 80 possessions per team, and player profiles pull small samples towards league level. Python for the simulator, React and three.js for the interface.

How I tested it

Every game of the men’s 2025–26 season was forecast from earlier data only, with settings locked on the previous season. Predicted and actual player points correlate at 0.65, with an average error of about 4.6 points.

What didn’t ship

Tuned priors scored worse (Brier 0.2400 against 0.2382). Game-to-game form made player ranges more honest, lifting 80% range coverage from 73% to 78%, but worsened game forecasts. Neither went into production. The women’s model beats chance but not the simple models (0.210 against 0.195).

Result

The more complex model didn’t beat the simple margin model, and I reported it as it is.

My rule: lock every model version before testing it and report the result, even when a simpler model wins.

From a generic dashboard to its own identity

Problem

The first interface felt like a generic AI-generated dashboard. Competing card colours made the analysis harder to read.

What I did

Green carries the brand and orange guides comparisons. Headline figures, units and context got clear roles, and the home page now uses the same working overview component as the app.

Rights

Real player photos looked good, but they carried rights belonging to photographers, players and clubs. I removed them and drew dot-matrix figures in code instead.

Design system

The first design system lives in Paper with matching tokens and components in code, so the coding agent can read my designs through Paper MCP and build against them.

Performance people feel on real phones

Problem

PageSpeed scored the mobile home page 38, with 20 seconds of blocking time.

What I did

The agent removed one visual effect at a time and measured again. Its first report led with no-GPU figures; I pushed back and ranked the work by what readers feel on real phones. A 730 kB shader drawing almost invisible fog was the real cost, so phones now get a static haze.

Process

Each fix went into its own pull request with before and after figures and a note on what it didn’t fix. Comparing screenshots of 13 page types at both widths caught a desktop fix that had broken the phone layout. The 3.8 s LCP is still above the 2.5 s target, and that is the next ticket.

Outcome

  • 38 → 71 mobile score
  • 10.4 s → 3.8 s Largest Contentful Paint
  • 20.4 s → 480 ms blocking time
  • 91 → 100 accessibility

Make the content findable beyond the app

What I did

Page-specific metadata, canonical URLs, a sitemap and pre-rendered summaries. Player and game pages carry their statistics, source links and Schema.org data in the HTML itself, so a search result can show real numbers rather than an empty app shell.

Next

Google has indexed the site. Next I want to see which searches lead to it, and whether AI answers cite it at all. I haven’t measured that yet.

From a tool to an argument

Question

Statistics pages answer questions people already have. I wanted to test whether the data could say something a fan might disagree with: what do five games tell you?

Finding

A team’s margin in its first five games correlated at 0.83 with the rest of the season in the women’s league, and at 0.28 in the men’s. With 43 team seasons, that is an observation, not a rule, and tests check the written claims against the data.

How I worked with agents

Split

Agents mapped which data was available and on what terms, then built the views. My part was deciding what good looks like and checking the result.

Memory

When I reject something, such as green scores on the match cards, the reason goes into an instruction file the next agent reads, so the idea doesn’t come back.

Understanding the code

I direct most of the code rather than write it, and I want to be able to explain it. So I had an agent build me a practice site around KorisLab for SQL, Python and spotting AI-written queries that run but give the wrong answer.
